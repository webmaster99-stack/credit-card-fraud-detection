## How it was chosen

Selected by the Phase 3 protocol (`docs/plan.md`): tune every ladder rung on train, score on
validation by the primary metric (recall at precision ≥ 0.50), calibrate, pick a threshold, compare
the top two models' v1 vs v2 feature sets, then evaluate once on test. Full ladder results in
`reports/model_ladder.json`.

| Model | Features | Recall @ P≥0.50 (valid) | PR-AUC (valid) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dummy (always legit) | v1 | 0.000 | 0.004 |
| Amount-rule heuristic | v1 | 0.000 | 0.153 |
| Logistic regression | v1 | 0.312 | 0.265 |
| Logistic regression + splines/interactions | v1 | 0.266 | 0.292 |
| Random forest | v1 | 0.892 | 0.842 |
| LightGBM | v1 | 0.915 | 0.874 |
| XGBoost | v1 | 0.921 | 0.888 |
| Isolation Forest (unsupervised) | v1 | 0.002 | 0.025 |
| XGBoost | v2 | 0.986 | 0.978 |
| **LightGBM** | **v2** | **0.990** | **0.978** |

Card-history (v2) features are responsible for almost all of the gain over the best v1 model
(XGBoost v1, recall 0.921): both XGBoost and LightGBM jump to ~0.99 recall once they can see a
card's recent velocity. The global SHAP summary (`reports/shap_summary.json`) backs this up directly
— behind `log_amt`, the next several most important features are v2 velocity features
(`log_spend_24h`, `txn_count_24h`, `hours_since_last_txn`, `txn_count_168h`, `amt_vs_card_mean`).
The spline/interaction logistic model did **not** beat plain logistic regression on the primary
metric (0.266 vs 0.312) despite a higher PR-AUC — a rung that didn't earn its complexity. Isolation
Forest is close to useless on this task: with 0.5% fraud spread across mostly one-hot categorical
dimensions, "anomalous" and "fraudulent" are not the same thing here, and the labels are doing
almost all of the work.

## Performance

| Split | Precision | Recall | PR-AUC | Expected cost* | TP | FP | FN | TN |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Validation | 0.500 | 0.990 | 0.978 | 0.0053 | 1,065 | 1,065 | 11 | 241,999 |
| **Test** (single evaluation) | **0.446** (CI 0.423–0.468) | **0.986** (CI 0.978–0.993) | **0.973** (CI 0.965–0.980) | 0.0050 | 923 | 1,146 | 13 | 279,439 |

*Mean per-transaction cost, a missed fraud weighted 20× a false alarm.

**Recall generalizes well** from validation to test (0.990 → 0.986, well within the confidence
interval). **Precision does not**: it falls from the validation target of 0.500 to 0.446 on test,
and the confidence interval (0.423–0.468) does not reach 0.50. This is a genuine, if modest,
generalization gap rather than noise — most likely some combination of (a) fraud prevalence
dropping from 0.44% (validation) to 0.33% (test), which mechanically lowers precision at a fixed
recall target, and (b) the usual risk of picking a threshold from a single validation curve. It is
reported honestly here rather than patched by re-tuning, since the test split is now spent for this
champion. The false-alarm rate in absolute terms is still low (0.41% of legitimate test
transactions), and every one of the 936 test frauds either gets caught (923) or missed by a very
small margin — see error analysis below.

### Why test precision is below 0.50: prevalence, not the operating point

| Split | Fraud prevalence | Recall (TPR) | False-alarm rate (FPR) | Precision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Validation | 0.441% | 0.990 | 0.438% | 0.500 |
| Test | 0.332% | 0.986 | 0.408% | 0.446 |

Precision is `TPR·π / (TPR·π + FPR·(1−π))` for fraud prevalence `π`, so at a fixed operating point it
falls when fraud gets rarer. Recall held and the false-alarm rate on legitimate transactions
slightly improved from validation to test, so the threshold generalized; what changed is the base
rate. Applying the validation TPR and FPR to the test prevalence gives precision 0.430, so the
observed 0.446 is a little better than the base-rate effect alone predicts. A fixed "precision ≥ 0.50"
target therefore cannot be guaranteed in a period with less fraud, whatever the model. Decision
(owner, 2026-10-01): the target stays a validation threshold rule, which is met; the champion and
threshold are unchanged, and nothing was re-tuned against the spent test split. For monitoring, the
false-alarm rate is the more stable quantity to watch than precision.

## Error analysis

13 of 936 test frauds were missed; 1,146 legitimate transactions were false alarms. Full samples in
`reports/error_analysis.json`.

- **Every missed fraud and every sampled false alarm scores within a hair of the decision threshold**
  (~0.0003, against a threshold of 0.00031) — these are not confident misses, they are coin-flip
  cases sitting right on the boundary. The model is not confidently wrong anywhere sampled.
- **Missed frauds skew toward small amounts in everyday categories**: `kids_pets` and
  `personal_care` account for over half the sample, most under $60 (one outlier at $866.79 in
  `shopping_pos`). These look like ordinary transactions with no obvious tell.
- **False alarms include several large, legitimate purchases in `shopping_net`/`shopping_pos`**
  ($3,120.74, $1,957.50, $1,407.94, $920.87, $900.01) — categories and amount ranges that overlap
  with genuine fraud patterns in this data (`docs/data_cards/sparkov.md` notes fraud concentrates in
  `shopping_net`/`misc_net`/`grocery_pos` and above a $200 amount band).

## Fairness

Recall (of actual fraud) and false-alarm rate (of legitimate transactions), by group, on test
(`reports/fairness.json`):

| Age band | n | Recall | False-alarm rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 18–24 | 22,495 | 1.000 | 0.32% |
| 25–34 | 57,762 | 0.993 | 0.42% |
| 35–44 | 60,745 | 0.988 | 0.45% |
| 45–54 | 59,481 | 0.980 | 0.45% |
| 55–64 | 34,784 | 0.994 | 0.41% |
| 65+ | 46,254 | 0.968 | 0.33% |

| Gender | n | Recall | False-alarm rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F | 154,589 | 0.979 | 0.50% |
| M | 126,932 | 0.993 | 0.30% |

Recall is fairly close across age bands (0.968–1.000); the 65+ band is lowest, worth watching as the
customer base evolves. The gender gap is more notable: recall is 1.4 points lower for F than M
(0.979 vs 0.993), and F's false-alarm rate is about 65% higher (0.50% vs 0.30%) — women's legitimate
transactions are more likely to be flagged. Neither gap is large in absolute terms, but they're
directionally consistent (worse on both recall and false-alarm rate for the same group), which is
worth a closer look before this model reaches production; the small sample sizes involved (11–13
missed frauds total) mean small count changes swing these ratios noticeably.

## Explainability

Global feature importance (mean |SHAP value|, 5,000-row validation sample,
`reports/shap_summary.json`): `log_amt` dominates, followed by `log_spend_24h`, `is_night`,
`txn_count_24h`, `category_gas_transport`, `hours_since_last_txn`, `txn_count_168h`,
`category_shopping_pos`, `age`, `category_shopping_net`. Per-prediction SHAP values and a
plain-language reason generator are in `src/fraud/explain/shap_values.py`, for reuse by the Gradio
demo and API in Phases 4–5.

## Known limitations

- **This is simulated data** (`docs/data_cards/sparkov.md`): the task is easier than real fraud
  detection, and these metrics will not reproduce on real cardholder data. The Phase 7 ULB benchmark
  is the realism check.
- **v2 needs an online card-history store.** This champion depends on velocity features computed
  from a card's transaction history; Phase 4's demo ships the simpler v1 (stateless) pipeline first,
  and Phase 5's API needs the Postgres-backed history store (`docs/adr/0003-feature-pipeline-design.md`)
  before this exact model can serve live traffic.
- **Precision on test (0.446) is below the project's 0.50 target.** See Performance, above.
- **Gender and age fairness gaps** noted above are worth monitoring, not yet mitigated.
